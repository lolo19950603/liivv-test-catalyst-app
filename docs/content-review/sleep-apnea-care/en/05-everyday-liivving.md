## Card 1

*Group `cleanReplace` · ask `provider` · figures: `columns`*

- Your cleaning routine `card 1.title`
- Your owner’s manual comes first. It tells you how to care for your model `card 1.items.1`
- Clean your mask every day. Wash it with pure soap and warm water `card 1.items.2`
- Skip soaps with bleach, chlorine, alcohol, moisturizers, scents or antibacterial agents `card 1.items.3`
- Rinse the mask in plain water. You can add plain white vinegar to the rinse water: it cuts down on smells and germs `card 1.items.4`
- Let the mask air dry. Keep it out of direct sunlight `card 1.items.5`
- Every week, hand wash the tubing and headgear with pure soap and warm water. Gently towel dry them, then let them air dry `card 1.items.6`
- If you use a humidifier, use distilled water and replace the water each night. Clean the humidifier as your manual says `card 1.items.7`
- Empty the humidifier before you move your machine, so water doesn’t spill into it `card 1.items.8`
- Wash your face and dry it well before you put the mask on `card 1.items.9`
- Don’t put a regular room humidifier in your bedroom: it could damage your CPAP machine’s motor. Ask your equipment provider how to keep your model clean and working well. `card 1.note`

- Ozone and UV cleaners `card 2.title`
- Clean your machine only with the methods in its instructions `card 2.items.1`
- For the machines in the 2021 Philips recall, the advice was to stop using cleaners that use ozone or ultraviolet (UV) light, because they aren’t authorized cleaning methods `card 2.items.2`
- In some recalled machines, ozone cleaning may make the sound-reducing foam inside break down faster `card 2.items.3`
- Breathing in ozone from ozone-generating devices may harm how your lungs work and irritate your airways `card 2.items.4`
- Have a machine in the Philips foam recall? Don’t stop or change how you use it on your own. Talk to your doctor or equipment provider first. Recalls are covered in Driving & Staying Safe. `card 2.note`

- Signs a part is worn `card 4.title`
- Check your tubing and headgear often. If they’re worn, get new ones `card 4.items.1`
- You shouldn’t have to pull the headgear very tight to stop leaks. If you can’t get a seal without large leaks, ask your provider about a different style of mask `card 4.items.2`
- Marks from the mask should fade soon after you take it off. If they don’t, or your skin is sore or red, the headgear may be too tight or the mask may not fit right `card 4.items.3`
- A small leak is fine, as long as it doesn’t blow into your eyes `card 4.items.4`
- Still sleepy even though you use CPAP every night? First make sure your equipment is working: your supplier can check it. Then talk to your sleep doctor `card 4.items.5`
- If you gain or lose 30 pounds or more, see your doctor. Your pressure may need to be changed, and a mask may no longer meet your needs `card 4.items.6`
- Machine not working properly? Call your provider. Equipment bought in Canada usually has a 3-year warranty. Ask about a loaner while yours is checked or repaired `card 4.items.7`
- You can report a problem with a medical device to Health Canada `card 4.items.8`
- Programs don’t pay to replace lost equipment or equipment damaged by misuse. Store and pack yours with care. `card 4.note`

## Card 5

*Group `cleanReplace` · ask `childProvider`*

- Caring for a child’s CPAP `card 5.title`
- Your child’s equipment provider (home care company) will give you instructions for your child’s machine. Follow those first `card 5.items.1`
- Clean your child’s mask each morning. Oils from your child’s skin can break down the mask `card 5.items.2`
- Wash the mask by hand with warm water and a mild soap. Let it air dry: don’t dry it with a towel or tissue `card 5.items.3`
- Wash the headgear by hand with mild soap and water once a week, and let it air dry `card 5.items.4`
- If the mask doesn’t seem to be a good size, ask the equipment provider about other masks your child could use `card 5.items.5`
- If an error message shows on your child’s CPAP machine, contact the equipment provider `card 5.items.6`
- If your child is sick and you’re worried about their breathing, contact your child’s health-care provider, or have your child seen at your local emergency department. `card 5.note`
- Sources: Positive airway pressure (PAP): CPAP and BPAP (AboutKidsHealth, SickKids)

## Card 6

*Group `cleanReplace` · ask `team` · figures: `columns`*

- Your machine’s data and follow-ups `card 6.title`
- Many machines record how many hours you use them, pauses in your breathing, and air leaks `card 6.items.1`
- Some send this to a phone app and to your equipment provider. Others save it to a USB stick or SD card, if you’d rather not use Wi-Fi, cellular data or Bluetooth `card 6.items.2`
- Many machines show an “events per hour” number. It’s a useful daily sign of how well treatment is working, but it isn’t the same as a sleep test result `card 6.items.3`
- New users may expect check-ins: about 7 days after starting, an overnight oxygen test at 2 to 4 weeks, then 30 and 60 days, with a review of the machine’s data `card 6.items.4`
- After that, a yearly follow-up with a download of your machine’s data is recommended `card 6.items.5`
- Ideally, you use your treatment every night, all night `card 6.items.6`
- Your doctor, insurer or a licensing office may ask for this data. If you’ve been told not to drive, Canadian guidance looks for at least 4 hours a night on more than 70% of nights, over at least 30 days. That is a licensing measure, not a health target: your sleep team sets your goal `card 6.items.7`
- Ask your provider how your data is shared and stored. Many machines store data on servers outside Canada, where privacy laws may be different `card 6.items.8`
- Your sleep team reads your data and decides on any changes. Only trained professionals should change your machine’s settings. Liivv never advises on them. `card 6.note`

## Card 7

*Group `away` · ask `provider` · figures: `columns`*

- Flying with CPAP `card 7.title`
- Your CPAP machine is allowed in carry-on and in checked baggage. Bring it as carry-on `card 7.items.1`
- At security, take the main unit out of its case and put it in a bin. You can put it in a clear plastic bag first: bring your own `card 7.items.2`
- The tubes and mask can stay in the case. Screening officers won’t touch the parts that connect to the tubes `card 7.items.3`
- The main unit may need extra screening, such as a swab test for explosives `card 7.items.4`
- Distilled water for your machine is exempt from the 100 ml liquid limit. Keep it in its own container and show it to the screening officer `card 7.items.5`
- You don’t need a medical document at security. If you think one would help, show it to the screening officer with your machine `card 7.items.6`
- Look for the Family and Accessible Line at security. If you have special medical needs, you can contact CATSA at least 48 hours before your flight `card 7.items.7`
- Tell your airline about your machine when you book. Ask about using it on board and about power `card 7.items.8`
- The usual limit of two carry-on bags doesn’t apply to medical equipment. Check your airline’s rules too `card 7.items.9`
- A letter from your doctor can make security go more smoothly. It should say your CPAP is needed for a medical condition, and list the model and serial number of all your equipment. `card 7.note`
