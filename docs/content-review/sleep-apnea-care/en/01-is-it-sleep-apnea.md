## Card 1

*Group `theSigns` · ask `doctor` · figures: `columns`*

- What sleep apnea is `card 1.title`
- Sleep apnea means short pauses in your breathing while you sleep `card 1.items.1`
- A pause usually lasts 10 to 30 seconds. Then your brain wakes you just enough to take a breath. Most people don’t remember waking `card 1.items.2`
- Obstructive sleep apnea is the most common type. Your airway narrows or closes while you sleep, even though your body keeps trying to breathe `card 1.items.3`
- Central sleep apnea is less common. Your airway is open, but your brain doesn’t send the signal to breathe `card 1.items.4`
- Untreated, sleep apnea can lead to daytime sleepiness and trouble thinking clearly. It raises the risk of car crashes, high blood pressure, heart attack and stroke `card 1.items.5`
- Sleep apnea can be treated. Your Treatment Options covers the choices `card 1.items.6`
- Children can have sleep apnea too. In the day, it can show up as trouble concentrating, hyperactivity or irritability. See This Might Be You. `card 1.note`

## Card 3

*Group `theSigns` · ask `doctor`*

- How common it is `card 3.title`
- In 2016 and 2017, 6.4% of Canadians said a health professional had diagnosed them with sleep apnea `card 3.items.1`
- Among people aged 60 to 79, it was 12.8%, about three times as common as in younger adults `card 3.items.2`
- Men were twice as likely as women to report a diagnosis `card 3.items.3`
- Women’s signs are more often tiredness or trouble sleeping, and men’s more often snoring. So sleep apnea may be missed more often in women `card 3.items.4`
- Nearly a third of Canadian adults (30%) were at moderate or high risk, based on a short questionnaire and body measurements `card 3.items.5`
- Many people have sleep apnea without knowing it. In population studies, 86% to 95% of people with significant sleep apnea had never been diagnosed `card 3.items.6`
- If any of this sounds like you, the checklist below can help you talk to your doctor. `card 3.note`
- Sources: Sleep apnea in Canada, 2016 and 2017 (archived page) (Statistics Canada); Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA): Assessment and Management in Adults (Government of BC, Guidelines and Protocols Advisory Committee); Sleep apnea (Canadian Lung Association)

- Trouble falling or staying asleep? `card 5.title`
- Trouble falling asleep, staying asleep or waking too early, with distress or daytime problems for 3 months or more, is called insomnia disorder `card 5.items.1`
- Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the first-line treatment, before sleeping pills `card 5.items.2`
- CBT-I is usually 4 to 8 sessions with a trained clinician, in person or by video. Some people start with a self-guided program, online or from a book `card 5.items.3`
- Sleepwell, a not-for-profit program led from Dalhousie University, has CBT-I tools such as a sleep diary `card 5.items.4`
- Poor sleep can also come from sleep apnea. A sleep study may be needed if your doctor suspects it `card 5.items.5`
- Adults aged 18 to 64 need 7 to 9 hours of sleep a night. Adults 65 and older need 7 to 8 `card 5.items.6`
- This site doesn’t cover insomnia in depth. Your doctor or nurse practitioner can assess it with you. `card 5.note`

## Card 7

*Group `gettingTested` · ask `doctor` · figures: `takeIn`*

- Talking to your doctor `card 7.title`
- If you think you have sleep apnea, see your family doctor or nurse practitioner `card 7.items.1`
- Expect questions about your sleep, your daytime sleepiness and your health, and a look at your nose, mouth and throat `card 7.items.2`
- You may be asked to fill in a short questionnaire about daytime sleepiness `card 7.items.3`
- Expect to be asked whether you drive for work or have a safety-critical job, and about any near misses at the wheel `card 7.items.4`
- Then your doctor orders a home sleep test or refers you to a sleep clinic. How this works differs by province `card 7.items.5`
- Waits can be long. The Canadian target (2011) is testing within 4 weeks of referral for suspected severe sleep apnea, a safety-critical job or some serious health conditions, and within 6 months for everyone `card 7.items.6`
- In a 2009 survey, nearly 1 in 4 adults (23%) who had been told they had sleep apnea had never been referred for an overnight sleep test `card 7.items.7`
- Funding programs can have rules about testing. In Ontario, funding for a machine through the Assistive Devices Program starts with a doctor at an ADP-registered sleep clinic. The Non-Insured Health Benefits program doesn’t pay for sleep tests. Funding & Coverage has the details for your province. `card 7.note`

## Card 8

*Group `gettingTested` · ask `doctor` · figures: `columns`*

- Lab or home test? `card 8.title`
- An in-lab sleep study (polysomnography, or Level 1) is an overnight test in a sleep lab `card 8.items.1`
- Small wires on your head, face, chest and legs record your brain activity, breathing and oxygen as you sleep. Staff watch through the night `card 8.items.2`
- It’s the standard test. It can find every type of sleep apnea, and other sleep problems `card 8.items.3`
- A home sleep apnea test is a small device you wear overnight in your own bed, after someone shows you how to put it on `card 8.items.4`
- It records your breathing, oxygen and pulse. It can’t measure sleep, so it’s less sensitive than a lab study `card 8.items.5`
- It checks only for obstructive sleep apnea, in adults with symptoms whose doctor thinks it’s likely `card 8.items.6`
- Home tests aren’t for checking people who have no symptoms `card 8.items.7`
- A negative or unclear home test doesn’t rule out sleep apnea. If your doctor still suspects it, a lab study is the next step `card 8.items.8`
- Overnight oximetry, a clip that measures oxygen and pulse, isn’t enough on its own to diagnose sleep apnea `card 8.items.9`
- Your doctor chooses the test. Serious heart or lung disease, a past stroke, other sleep problems, regular opioid use and some other conditions mean a home test isn’t the right test. Your doctor refers you to a sleep specialist instead `card 8.items.10`
- For children, an overnight lab study is the standard. A home test is a second choice, only for healthy children over 5 who can’t get a lab study. More in This Might Be You. `card 8.note`

## Card 9

*Group `gettingTested` · ask `sleepTeam` · figures: `ahiScale`*

- Your results: the AHI `card 9.title`
- Your sleep study report says whether you have sleep apnea, and whether it’s mild, moderate or severe `card 9.items.1`
- The main number is the AHI (apnea-hypopnea index): how many times an hour your breathing stops or becomes shallow `card 9.items.2`
- Under 5 an hour is normal `card 9.items.3`
- Mild is 5 to 14 an hour, moderate is 15 to 29, and severe is 30 or more `card 9.items.4`
- The AHI isn’t the whole picture. Your sleep doctor also weighs your symptoms, your health and your job `card 9.items.5`
- Treatment may be suggested even when the AHI is mild, if you’re tired or sleepy `card 9.items.6`
- Once you’re on CPAP, the machine shows “events per hour”. It’s a useful daily guide, but not a true AHI. Ask your sleep team what yours means `card 9.items.7`
- This scale helps you read a report, not test yourself. Your sleep doctor goes over your results with you. `card 9.note`

## Card 10

*Group `gettingTested` · ask `sleepTeam` · figures: `columns`*

- What happens next `card 10.title`
- A sleep doctor or your own doctor reads your results, makes the diagnosis and suggests a treatment `card 10.items.1`
- The choice depends on how severe it is, your symptoms, your health and your job `card 10.items.2`
- CPAP is generally the most effective treatment. A machine blows a steady stream of air through a mask, to keep your airway open `card 10.items.3`
- Other options include an oral appliance for mild to moderate sleep apnea. Healthy eating, exercise and weight loss are recommended for people who carry extra weight, but they aren’t enough on their own if you’re sleepy or your sleep apnea is moderate or severe. Your Treatment Options covers each one `card 10.items.4`
- You need a prescription for CPAP. Your prescriber sets your settings `card 10.items.5`
- Expect follow-up: soon after you start, to check the treatment is working, then over the long term `card 10.items.6`
- Starting CPAP walks you through setup and the first weeks. `card 10.note`
