## `machineGuide`

- Common sleep apnea machines, compared `machineGuide.title`
- Liivv doesn’t sell CPAP or other sleep apnea machines yet. `machineGuide.notSold`
- This guide helps you talk to your prescriber about the machines in it. It doesn’t recommend one. `machineGuide.intro`
- Machine types, and who prescribes each `machineGuide.cards.1.title`
- A CPAP machine blows air at one steady pressure `machineGuide.cards.1.items.1`
- An auto CPAP (APAP) changes the pressure through the night as your breathing pattern changes `machineGuide.cards.1.items.2`
- A bilevel (BiPAP) machine uses a higher pressure when you breathe in and a lower one when you breathe out `machineGuide.cards.1.items.3`
- Some bilevel machines are more advanced and are made for complex breathing disorders. They aren’t needed for obstructive sleep apnea. ASV is one of these (see the ASV card) `machineGuide.cards.1.items.4`
- Every machine needs a prescription. Some provincial guidance says auto and bilevel machines should be prescribed only by a sleep specialist `machineGuide.cards.1.items.5`
- Each CPAP machine must be set for the person using it, and only trained professionals should change machine settings `machineGuide.cards.1.items.6`
- Travel machines `machineGuide.cards.2.title`
- Travel machines are easier to take with you, and some run on a battery `machineGuide.cards.2.items.1`
- The trade-offs: pressure may not be as steady, batteries cost extra when there’s no wall power, and they may not give enough humidity `machineGuide.cards.2.items.2`
- Altitude can change how a machine works unless it has features that adjust for it. Talk about portability and altitude with your prescriber and your equipment supplier `machineGuide.cards.2.items.3`
- If you plan to use your machine on the plane, tell the airline’s reservation agent whether it uses batteries or needs onboard power `machineGuide.cards.2.items.4`
- When you fly, bring your machine as carry-on with a letter from your doctor, and check the type of power supply where you’re going `machineGuide.cards.2.items.5`
- Some travel machines take only their own masks and parts, so check before you buy a mask `machineGuide.cards.2.items.6`
- Data and apps `machineGuide.cards.3.title`
- Many machines record how many hours you use them, breathing events and mask leak `machineGuide.cards.3.items.1`
- Some connect to a phone app by Bluetooth, Wi-Fi or cellular, so you can track your data and send reports to your provider. Others store reports on an SD or USB card, if you’d rather not use a wireless connection `machineGuide.cards.3.items.2`
- Many machines store data on servers outside Canada, where privacy laws may differ. Ask your provider how they report your data and how they keep it safe `machineGuide.cards.3.items.3`
- The chart shows how each machine shares data. `machineGuide.cards.3.note`
- Recalls and safety `machineGuide.cards.4.title`
- PAP machines sold in Canada must have a Health Canada medical device licence. You can look up a model in Health Canada’s Medical Devices Active Licence Listing (MDALL), and check Health Canada’s website for recalls `machineGuide.cards.4.items.1`
- The Philips foam recall (RA-62729) covers several Philips CPAP and bilevel machines and ventilators made before April 26, 2021. If you have one, talk to your doctor or equipment provider before you stop or change how you use it `machineGuide.cards.4.items.2`
- Clean it only as the maker’s instructions say. Cleaning methods that haven’t been validated, such as ozone, could add to the foam breakdown `machineGuide.cards.4.items.3`
- The licences in this guide were checked on 2026-10-13. Licences and recalls change, so check again before you buy. `machineGuide.cards.4.note`
- Search MDALL `machineGuide.cards.4.links.mdall`
- Search recalls and safety alerts `machineGuide.cards.4.links.recalls`
- Questions to ask before you buy `machineGuide.cards.5.title`
- Is this the type of machine my prescriber ordered, and is it right for my condition? `machineGuide.cards.5.items.1`
- Does it have a Health Canada licence, and has it been recalled? `machineGuide.cards.5.items.2`
- Is it new or used, and is there a warranty? Equipment bought in Canada usually has a 3-year warranty and should have a CSA stamp `machineGuide.cards.5.items.3`
- Will the seller ask for my prescription before selling it to me? `machineGuide.cards.5.items.4`
- Who sets it up, follows up with me, and handles repairs? `machineGuide.cards.5.items.5`
- Can I try it first? Some suppliers offer trials, and some don’t give returns or refunds once equipment has been used `machineGuide.cards.5.items.6`
- With a second-hand machine, it can be hard to know if it works well and if any warranty is left `machineGuide.cards.5.items.7`
- Talk to your doctor before and after you buy. `machineGuide.cards.5.note`
- ASV `machineGuide.cards.asv.title`
- ASV is a type of bilevel machine. Some provincial guidance says bilevel machines should be prescribed only by a sleep specialist `machineGuide.cards.asv.items.1`
- People with suspected central sleep apnea should be checked with a detailed overnight test in a sleep lab to find the right treatment `machineGuide.cards.asv.items.2`
- In 2015, Health Canada posted a Type I recall of several ASV machines. It names a serious safety concern for people with symptomatic, chronic heart failure with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction who are on ASV therapy: a 33.5% increased risk of cardiovascular death `machineGuide.cards.asv.items.3`
- Ontario’s Assistive Devices Program doesn’t cover ASV `machineGuide.cards.asv.items.4`
