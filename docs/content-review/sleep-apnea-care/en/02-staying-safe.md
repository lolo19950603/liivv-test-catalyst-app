## Card 2

*Group `road` · ask `doctor`*

- Who decides if you can drive `card 2.title`
- Your doctor assesses whether you’re medically fit to drive `card 2.items.1`
- The licensing authority where you live decides whether you can hold a licence. Driving safety is a provincial matter, not a federal one `card 2.items.2`
- How severe your sleep apnea is doesn’t decide it on its own. Sleepiness, other health conditions, medicines, your sleep schedule and past crashes count too `card 2.items.3`
- The rules differ from province to province `card 2.items.4`
- In Ontario, doctors and nurse practitioners must report sleep apnea that is untreated or not successfully treated, with an AHI of 30 or more and daytime sleepiness. A mandatory report leads to a licence suspension `card 2.items.5`
- In BC, drivers with sleep apnea have a licence condition: stop driving and report any episode of sleep at the wheel to their doctor and to RoadSafetyBC `card 2.items.6`
- AHI is the number of breathing pauses and shallow breaths per hour of sleep. Chapter 01 explains it. Ask your doctor how the rules apply to you. `card 2.note`
- Sources: Canadian Thoracic Society and Canadian Sleep Society position paper: Obstructive sleep apnea and driving (CTS, CSS); Reporting a driver for medical review (Government of Ontario, Ministry of Transportation); Medical Condition Report (Form 5108E) (Ontario Ministry of Transportation); 18 - Sleep disorders - CCMTA Medical Standards (RoadSafetyBC, Government of BC)

## Card 3

- What’s checked, by licence class `card 3.title`
- Every class: severe sleep apnea (AHI 30 or more) rules out driving unless it’s successfully treated, or a sleep specialist finds a low risk of a sleep-related crash `card 3.items.1`
- Every class: after a crash from falling asleep or sleepiness in the past 5 years, you must show your sleep apnea is successfully treated `card 3.items.2`
- In BC, treatment is checked with a full download from your CPAP or bilevel machine prepared by a respiratory therapist, or with a sleep study `card 3.items.3`
- Every class, if you use CPAP: machine records showing use of at least 4 hours a night, on more than 70% of nights, over at least 30 days `card 3.items.4`
- Private licences: your sleep doctor or your family doctor can confirm you’re fit to drive again `card 3.items.5`
- Commercial licences: the checks are stricter than for private drivers. Commercial drivers should ideally be seen at a sleep centre within 4 weeks of referral `card 3.items.6`
- Commercial licences: after a crash from falling asleep, or excessive sleepiness while driving, stop driving now until sleep tests are done and treatment works `card 3.items.7`

## Card 4

*Group `road` · ask `sleepTeam`*

- While you start treatment `card 4.title`
- While treatment starts, you may be able to keep driving if you aren’t sleepy while driving and haven’t had a crash from falling asleep. Ask your doctor `card 4.items.1`
- If you have been sleepy at the wheel or had a sleep-related crash, driving can start again once you use your treatment well and the sleepiness is gone `card 4.items.2`
- Your machine records how many hours and days you use it. Your doctor, an insurer or a licensing authority may ask for this report `card 4.items.3`
- Once treatment works, driving safely is your responsibility. Have your treatment reviewed from time to time by your doctor or sleep specialist, for example at least every 5 years `card 4.items.4`
- If sleepiness at the wheel comes back, stop driving and tell your doctor `card 4.items.5`
- Your machine’s settings are changed only by trained professionals. If your treatment isn’t working, call your sleep team. `card 4.note`

## Card 5

*Group `device` · ask `doctor`*

- Recalls: what to do `card 5.title`
- In 2021, Philips Respironics recalled several models of CPAP and bilevel machines. Sound-reducing foam inside them could break down, and particles could get into the air you breathe `card 5.items.1`
- If your machine is recalled, don’t stop or change your therapy before you talk to your doctor or equipment provider `card 5.items.2`
- Don’t try to remove or replace the foam yourself `card 5.items.3`
- Clean the machine only the way the maker’s instructions say. Ozone cleaning may make the foam break down faster `card 5.items.4`
- Some masks with magnets have also been recalled. See the next card `card 5.items.5`
- The maker planned to close its registration portal at the end of December 2024, and to finish its repair and replacement program in Canada by the end of June 2025. If you still use an affected machine, talk to your doctor or equipment provider. `card 5.note`

## Card 6

*Group `device` · ask `doctor` · urgent: pinned open, figures kept in every locale · figures: `columns`*

- Masks with magnets `card 6.title`
- Some CPAP masks have magnets in the clips or straps that hold the headgear on `card 6.items.1`
- Close to some metal implants and medical devices, a magnet may affect how they work or move them out of place. This could cause serious injury, or even death `card 6.items.2`
- It matters for everyone close to the mask: you, the people you live with, caregivers and bed partners `card 6.items.3`
- Implants that magnets may affect include pacemakers, defibrillators (ICDs), nerve stimulators (including hypoglossal nerve stimulators), brain fluid shunts, aneurysm clips, embolic coils, metal stents, implanted ports and pumps such as insulin pumps, hearing implants with a magnet such as cochlear implants, and metal in the eye. The full list is longer `card 6.items.4`
- What to do depends on the mask and on the implant. If you, or anyone close to the mask, has an implant or metal in their body, check with the mask’s maker and with your health care team about that implant before you use a mask with magnets `card 6.items.5`
- Some masks are named in Health Canada notices. For the masks in the 2022 public advisory, the advice is to stop using the mask if anyone close to it has one of the listed implants, and to get a mask without magnets. For an implant that isn’t on the list, keep the mask at least 15 cm (6 inches) away from it `card 6.items.6`
- For the masks in the 2022 advisory: if no one close to the mask has a metal implant or metal object in their body, you can keep using it `card 6.items.7`
- Not sure whether your mask has magnets? Check its instructions, or ask its maker. You can also look up your mask on the Government of Canada’s <link>recalls and safety alerts</link> site. `card 6.note`

## Card 7

*Group `device` · ask `provider`*

- Cleaning the approved way `card 7.title`
- Clean your machine and mask only the way the maker’s instructions say `card 7.items.1`
- For the machines in the 2021 Philips recall, the advice was to stop using cleaning methods the maker hasn’t approved, such as ozone or UV-light cleaners `card 7.items.2`
- Ozone cleaning can speed up the breakdown of the foam inside some machines `card 7.items.3`
- Breathing ozone may affect how well your lungs work. UV light may badly burn skin and eyes `card 7.items.4`
- There isn’t enough evidence that all ozone and UV devices sold to kill germs are safe to use, or that they work `card 7.items.5`
- Everyday cleaning with pure soap and warm water is in Chapter 05. `card 7.note`
- Sources: Philips Respironics recalls several models of CPAP and BiLevel PAP machines and mechanical ventilators (RA-62729) (Health Canada); Position statement from the Canadian Thoracic Society, Canadian Sleep Society and the Canadian Society of Respiratory Therapists: Philips Respironics device recall (CTS, CSS, CSRT; on css-scs.ca); Regulating ultraviolet radiation-emitting and ozone-generating devices under the Pest Control Products Act: Overview (Health Canada, Pest Management Regulatory Agency); Sleep apnea (Canadian Lung Association)

## Card 8

*Group `device` · ask `doctor`*

- Buying a device safely `card 8.title`
- Use CPAP only if you have a prescription from your doctor `card 8.items.1`
- Auto (APAP) and bilevel machines are more complex, and should be prescribed only by a sleep specialist `card 8.items.2`
- PAP machines sold in Canada must have a Health Canada medical device licence. You can check a device in the Medical Devices Active Licence Listing (MDALL) `card 8.items.3`
- Buying online can mean getting a device that is unlicensed, recalled or fake `card 8.items.4`
- Don’t buy from a website that won’t give a street address or a working phone number `card 8.items.5`
- Second-hand equipment: make sure it’s clean and works as it should. Every machine must be set for the person using it `card 8.items.6`
- Ask your health care provider whether a device is right for you before you buy, and follow up afterwards to check it fits and works. `card 8.note`
- Sources: Buyer's Information for CPAP Therapy Equipment (MyHealth Alberta); Positive Airway Pressure (PAP) Buyer's Guide (BC Ministry of Health); Buying Medical Devices from the Internet (Health Canada); Medical Devices Active Licence Listing (MDALL) - Your reference tool for licensed medical devices in Canada (Health Canada)

- Choose a supplier with a registered respiratory therapist or a registered sleep technologist to guide you `card 9.items.1`
