## Card 1

*Group `seasonsOfLife` · ask `doctor` · figures: `columns`*

- Signs in children `card 1.title`
- Sleep apnea affects 1 to 5 children in 100. Some children snore but don’t have sleep apnea `card 1.items.1`
- At night: snoring (steady, with pauses, or only in some positions), breathing only through the mouth, long pauses in breathing, waking often, and sweating `card 1.items.2`
- Restless sleep can look like turning from side to side, jerking the legs, or sitting up to breathe better while still asleep `card 1.items.3`
- During the day: sleepiness, especially at school or in quiet activities, trouble learning or concentrating, low energy for sports and hobbies, and irritability, hyperactivity, impulsivity or aggression `card 1.items.4`
- The most common cause is large tonsils or adenoids. This often happens between ages 2 and 8. Extra weight, weak breathing muscles, the shape of the skull and jaw, and other health conditions can also cause it `card 1.items.5`
- Other signs parents notice include bed-wetting, night terrors, and unusual sleep positions, such as the head tipped back or sitting up with the mouth open `card 1.items.6`
- Finding it early matters. Untreated, it can be linked to high blood pressure, poor growth, learning difficulties and behaviour problems `card 1.items.7`
- These signs are not a diagnosis. If you notice them, talk to your child’s doctor. Snoring and long pauses while your child sleeps are worth mentioning. `card 1.note`

## Card 2

*Group `seasonsOfLife` · ask `doctor` · figures: `takeIn`*

- Testing for children `card 2.title`
- Start with your child’s doctor. They will usually refer your child to a sleep medicine specialist at a hospital `card 2.items.1`
- An overnight sleep study in a sleep lab (polysomnography) is the standard test for children. There is no replacement for it at this time `card 2.items.2`
- During the study, small wires are attached to the skin with paste and child-friendly tape. They don’t hurt. They record oxygen, breathing, heartbeat and sleep, and a sleep technologist watches through the night `card 2.items.3`
- A home sleep test may be a second option for otherwise healthy children over 5, when a lab study is out of reach because of long waits, distance or cost `card 2.items.4`
- Overnight oximetry (a finger sensor that measures oxygen) and questionnaires can help, but they shouldn’t be used alone to diagnose sleep apnea in children `card 2.items.5`
- Lab studies are hard to get in many parts of Canada, and waits can delay treatment. Ask your child’s doctor what the wait is where you live `card 2.items.6`
- Your child’s sleep specialist looks at the results to find what kind of sleep problem it is and how serious it is. `card 2.note`

## Card 3 — HELD (`childTreatmentRuling`)

*Group `seasonsOfLife` · ask `childTeam` · urgent: pinned open, figures kept in every locale · figures: `columns`*

- Treatment for children `card 3.title`
- Surgery to remove the tonsils and adenoids (adenotonsillectomy) is often the first treatment, because large tonsils and adenoids are often the cause `card 3.items.1`
- Other treatments include a weight program if your child carries extra weight, medicines to reduce swelling in the nose or airway, and less exposure to second-hand smoke and other pollution `card 3.items.2`
- Some children need CPAP, a machine that blows air through a mask to keep the airway open. Some still need it after other treatments `card 3.items.3`
- After this surgery, children with sleep apnea are watched more closely in hospital. Most go home after one night. Children under 3 with other conditions, such as Down syndrome, are more likely to have breathing problems after surgery `card 3.items.4`
- Your child may need a follow-up sleep study after treatment `card 3.items.5`
- Once your child is home after surgery, call your child’s surgeon, clinic or doctor right away for a fever of 38.5°C or higher, vomiting that won’t stop, pain that gets worse, refusing to drink, or fresh blood in the nose or mouth `card 3.items.6`
- If your child is bleeding or having trouble breathing, take them to the closest emergency department right away `card 3.items.7`
- If your child has other health conditions, the sleep team may also bring in other specialists. Tonsil surgery isn’t used to treat snoring alone in children. `card 3.note`

## Card 4

*Group `seasonsOfLife` · ask `childTeam` · urgent: pinned open, figures kept in every locale · figures: `columns`*

- A child on CPAP `card 4.title`
- Your child’s sleep team follows your child’s care. A sleep study sets the pressure on the machine, and another study after starting checks that it’s working and whether the pressure needs to change `card 4.items.1`
- Your child needs a mask made for children (a paediatric mask), fitted and tried on, not an adult mask `card 4.items.2`
- CPAP and follow-up care are different for children. Get help from professionals who work with children `card 4.items.3`
- Your child can practise wearing the mask for short times during the day, during a quiet activity they enjoy `card 4.items.4`
- Most children need time to get used to it. Make it part of the bedtime routine. A sticker chart or other reward can help `card 4.items.5`
- Don’t fasten the mask too tightly. Check your child’s face for pressure marks `card 4.items.6`
- CPAP is for every night of sleep, including holidays, sleepovers and camping `card 4.items.7`
- If your child is sick and you’re worried about their breathing, call your child’s health-care provider or have your child seen at your local emergency department `card 4.items.8`
- Children’s CPAP gives one steady pressure, set by the sleep team. Auto-adjusting machines are not for children. Some masks with magnets, including a youth version of one mask, have had Health Canada warnings: see Partners and household. `card 4.note`

## Card 5

*Group `seasonsOfLife` · ask `sleepTeam` · figures: `columns`*

- Later life `card 5.title`
- In 2016 and 2017, 12.8% of Canadians aged 60 to 79 said they had been diagnosed with sleep apnea. That’s three times the rate in adults aged 18 to 59 `card 5.items.1`
- Adults aged 40 to 79 are at higher risk than younger adults. Being over 40 is one of the risk factors `card 5.items.2`
- As you get older, or if you gain weight, habits that helped before may stop being enough. If your symptoms come back, see your doctor `card 5.items.3`
- If you gain or lose 30 pounds or more, see your doctor. Your treatment may need to be checked `card 5.items.4`
- Before sedation or anesthesia, for a dental procedure, a test or surgery, tell everyone caring for you that you have sleep apnea `card 5.items.5`
- Use your CPAP or dental device everywhere you sleep, including an overnight stay in hospital. A medical alert bracelet that says you have sleep apnea can help `card 5.items.6`
- In Ontario, residents of long-term care homes can get Assistive Devices Program funding for a PAP machine `card 5.items.7`
- These numbers come from a national health survey. They describe Canadians in general, not any one person. `card 5.note`

## Card 6

*Group `seasonsOfLife` · ask `doctor`*

- Women: the signs can look different `card 6.title`
- In a 2016–2017 national survey, men were twice as likely as women to say they had been diagnosed with sleep apnea `card 6.items.1`
- Men more often have the classic signs, such as loud snoring or someone seeing them stop breathing `card 6.items.2`
- Women more often feel tired or sleepy in the day, or have trouble falling or staying asleep `card 6.items.3`
- Because the signs differ, sleep apnea may be missed in women `card 6.items.4`
- Always tired, or sleeping badly, and not sure why? Ask your doctor whether sleep apnea could be part of it. See Is It Sleep Apnea? for the signs. `card 6.note`
- Sources: Sleep apnea in Canada, 2016 and 2017 (archived page) (Statistics Canada)

## Card 7

*Group `careAndCommunity` · ask `provider` · urgent: pinned open, figures kept in every locale · figures: `columns`*

- Partners and household `card 7.title`
- Bed partners and family often notice the signs first. Many people with sleep apnea don’t know they snore or gasp for breath at night `card 7.items.1`
- Once your partner is treated, you may sleep better too `card 7.items.2`
- Some CPAP masks have magnets in the clips or straps. Their magnetic field may affect how some metal implants and objects in the body work, or move them. Examples are pacemakers, metal stents and metal in the eye. This may cause serious injury `card 7.items.3`
- For the masks in Health Canada’s 2022 advisory, the advice is to stop using the mask if you, anyone in your household, a caregiver or a bed partner has one of the listed implants, and to get a mask with no magnets. For other masks with magnets, check with the mask’s maker and your health care team `card 7.items.4`
- A later Health Canada recall of other masks with magnets warned that interference could affect how an implant works or where it sits, with a risk of serious injury or death `card 7.items.5`
- Each machine is set for the person using it. Using a machine set for someone else could harm your health `card 7.items.6`
- Does anyone in your home have a pacemaker, defibrillator, cochlear implant, metal stent, clip or plate, or any other implant? Check whether your mask has magnets, and check with the mask’s maker and your health care team before you use it. `card 7.note`

## Card 8

*Group `careAndCommunity` · ask `sleepTeam` · figures: `columns`*

- Helping someone `card 8.title`
- If someone you love snores with pauses or gasps in their sleep, and is very sleepy in the day, encourage them to see their doctor `card 8.items.1`
- Learn about their prescribed treatment, and help them get used to it `card 8.items.2`
- Encourage them to keep going. Getting comfortable with a mask can take a few weeks, or even months `card 8.items.3`
- Reassure them that using CPAP doesn’t change how you feel about them `card 8.items.4`
- Support their efforts to be active, lose weight or quit smoking `card 8.items.5`
- Helping a parent? Before a hospital stay or sedation, make sure the care team knows they have sleep apnea, and pack their machine `card 8.items.6`
- Women more often have tiredness or trouble sleeping as signs, so listen to how they feel, not only to how they sound `card 8.items.7`
- Falling asleep at the wheel, or a near miss, can’t wait. See the warning signs at the top of Driving & Staying Safe. `card 8.note`

## Card 9 — HELD (`indigenousPartnerReview`: title and held note only)

*Group `careAndCommunity` · ask `nihb` · figures: `columns`*

- First Nations and Inuit: NIHB coverage `card 9.title`
- The Non-Insured Health Benefits (NIHB) program covers CPAP and auto CPAP machines, masks and supplies for eligible First Nations and Inuit. Prior approval is needed `card 9.items.1`
- You can be eligible if you live in Canada and are a First Nations person registered under the Indian Act, an Inuk recognized by an Inuit land claim organization, or a child under 2 whose parent is eligible `card 9.items.2`
- Some people get these benefits through a First Nations or Inuit government, community or health authority instead, such as clients of the First Nations Health Authority in BC. Not sure? Contact your NIHB regional office `card 9.items.3`
- The request needs a diagnosis of sleep apnea with symptoms, your sleep study results and a prescription. It can start with a rental of up to 3 months, or with a request to buy. Buying needs test results that show your sleep improved on the machine `card 9.items.4`
- NIHB’s replacement guidelines allow up to: a machine every 5 years, and 2 masks with headgear, 2 tubes and 14 inlet filters a year. Each replacement needs a new prescription `card 9.items.5`
- Equipment comes from an enrolled respiratory provider with a registered respiratory therapist, or a registered nurse trained in managing respiratory conditions, on staff. The price covers mask fitting, set-up, follow-up and teaching `card 9.items.6`
- NIHB doesn’t cover the cost of the sleep test itself `card 9.items.7`
- Coverage is only for goods and services you get in Canada. Show your provider your client identification so they can confirm your coverage. `card 9.note`
- Coverage for First Nations and Inuit is being reviewed with an Indigenous health partner and will appear here soon. Until then, contact your NIHB regional office. `card 9.heldNote`

## Card 10

*Group `careAndCommunity` · ask `provider` · figures: `columns`*

- Paying for it `card 10.title`
- You can claim a CPAP machine bought with a prescription as a medical expense on your income tax return `card 10.items.1`
- Many insurance plans cover CPAP equipment prescribed by a sleep doctor. Your insurer will ask for a copy of your prescription `card 10.items.2`
- Public coverage differs by province. For example, Alberta’s public health insurance doesn’t currently pay for CPAP equipment, while Ontario has a program that helps pay for a machine `card 10.items.3`
- In Ontario, the Assistive Devices Program pays 75% of its price for a PAP machine, or 100% if you get Ontario Works, the Ontario Disability Support Program or Assistance for Children with Severe Disabilities. It doesn’t pay for replacement masks or headgear `card 10.items.4`
- For Ontario’s program, you must buy from a business registered with it, and follow its steps before you buy. If you buy first, you pay the full cost `card 10.items.5`
- Veterans Affairs Canada’s respiratory equipment benefit includes CPAP, auto CPAP and bilevel machines, for eligible clients `card 10.items.6`
- The Canadian Lung Association has a grant program to help pay for children’s CPAP machines `card 10.items.7`
- First Nations or Inuit? See NIHB coverage above. Keep your receipts and your prescription. Insurers ask for a copy of your prescription, and receipts support a medical expense claim on your tax return. `card 10.note`
