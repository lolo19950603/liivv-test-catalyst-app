## Card 1

*Group `machines` · ask `team` · figures: `columns`*

- CPAP and APAP: the usual first treatment `card 1.title`
- CPAP is generally the most effective treatment for obstructive sleep apnea `card 1.items.1`
- A machine blows a steady stream of air through a tube into a mask. The air holds your airway open while you sleep `card 1.items.2`
- CPAP gives one steady pressure all night. APAP (auto CPAP) changes the pressure through the night as your breathing changes `card 1.items.3`
- You need a prescription. Your prescriber sets the pressure, and only trained professionals should change the machine’s settings `card 1.items.4`
- Each machine is set for the person using it. Using a machine set for someone else could harm your health `card 1.items.5`
- CPAP is a treatment, not a cure. It only works on the nights you use it `card 1.items.6`
- Fixed CPAP is the main treatment. APAP is an alternative, but not for people with heart or lung disease, nerve or muscle disease, or breathing problems in sleep that aren’t only obstructive, such as central sleep apnea. APAP is more complex than CPAP, and some guidance says only a sleep specialist should prescribe it. Your prescriber decides which one you get. `card 1.note`

## Card 2

*Group `machines` · ask `team`*

- Bilevel (BiPAP) `card 2.title`
- A bilevel machine gives a higher pressure when you breathe in and a lower pressure when you breathe out `card 2.items.1`
- For obstructive sleep apnea, the main reason to use one is comfort: some people find it easier to breathe out against a lower pressure `card 2.items.2`
- Bilevel machines are more complex than CPAP. They should be prescribed only by a sleep specialist `card 2.items.3`
- Bilevel is also used for some other breathing problems in sleep, when oxygen and carbon dioxide levels don’t improve with CPAP `card 2.items.4`
- Government funding may help some people pay for bilevel. Ask your sleep team, and see Paying for it `card 2.items.5`
- Canadian guidance differs on when bilevel is used for obstructive sleep apnea. Your sleep specialist decides whether it’s right for you. `card 2.note`
- Sources: Positive Airway Pressure (PAP) Buyer's Guide (BC Ministry of Health); Buyer's Information for CPAP Therapy Equipment (MyHealth Alberta); Sleep apnea (Canadian Lung Association); Sleep apnea (Lung Health Foundation); Canadian Thoracic Society 2011 guideline update: Diagnosis and treatment of sleep disordered breathing (Canadian Thoracic Society)

## Card 3

*Group `machines` · ask `team`*

- Central and complex sleep apnea: a specialist’s call `card 3.title`
- In central sleep apnea, the throat and airway are normal. The brain doesn’t send the signal to breathe `card 3.items.1`
- It is common in people with heart failure, atrial fibrillation or other heart conditions, people taking opioids, and people with some neurological conditions `card 3.items.2`
- Standard PAP machines are not meant to be the only treatment for central sleep apnea. Used alone, they can make it worse `card 3.items.3`
- If central sleep apnea is suspected, it should be checked with a detailed overnight sleep test in an accredited sleep lab, to find the right treatment `card 3.items.4`
- Treating the other condition, such as heart failure, can improve central sleep apnea `card 3.items.5`
- Some people start having central pauses after they begin CPAP. This is called complex sleep apnea, and it can happen in up to 10% of people treated with CPAP. If it happens to you, talk with your doctor about continuing PAP `card 3.items.6`
- Some newer machines, such as adaptive servo-ventilation (ASV), are used for central sleep apnea. In 2015, Health Canada posted a Type I recall of several ASV machines: a serious safety concern was found in people with symptomatic, chronic heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, whose risk of cardiovascular death was 33.5% higher on ASV. ASV is a sleep specialist’s decision. If you have heart failure, make sure your sleep specialist knows. `card 3.note`
- Sources: Sleep apnea (Canadian Lung Association); Sleep apnea (Lung Health Foundation); Positive Airway Pressure (PAP) Buyer's Guide (BC Ministry of Health); Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA): Assessment and Management in Adults (Government of BC, Guidelines and Protocols Advisory Committee); Canadian Thoracic Society 2011 guideline update: Diagnosis and treatment of sleep disordered breathing (Canadian Thoracic Society); Adaptive Servo-Ventilation (ASV) therapy devices (RA-53771) (Health Canada)

## Card 4

*Group `machines` · ask `provider` · figures: `columns`*

- Travel machines `card 4.title`
- Smaller travel machines are easy to carry, quiet, and can run on batteries `card 4.items.1`
- The trade-offs: the pressure may not stay as steady, batteries cost extra where there’s no power outlet, and they may not humidify the air enough `card 4.items.2`
- Altitude can change how a machine works, unless it adjusts for it. Tell your provider if you live in, or often travel to, places much higher or lower than where your pressure was set `card 4.items.3`
- Most machines can also run on a battery pack, a car adapter or a marine battery adapter `card 4.items.4`
- A travel machine still needs a prescription, set by your prescriber for you `card 4.items.5`
- Keep using your treatment when you travel. Flying? See Everyday Liivving for taking your machine on board `card 4.items.6`
- Talk to your prescriber and equipment provider before you choose a travel machine, so it fits your prescription and the way you travel. `card 4.note`

## Card 5

*Group `machines` · ask `provider` · figures: `containers`*

- Renting, trying or buying `card 5.title`
- You can buy or rent CPAP equipment `card 5.items.1`
- Ask about a trial. Some suppliers offer equipment trials, and some won’t take back or refund equipment once it has been used `card 5.items.2`
- If you end up needing a different machine, an extended health insurer may refuse to pay for a second one. That’s one more reason a trial matters `card 5.items.3`
- Look for a supplier with a registered respiratory therapist or a registered polysomnographic technologist to support you `card 5.items.4`
- Equipment bought in Canada usually has a 3-year warranty. Ask about warranty, how long repairs take, and whether you get a loaner in the meantime `card 5.items.5`
- Every PAP machine sold in Canada needs a Health Canada medical device licence. You can check a machine in Health Canada’s licence list `card 5.items.6`
- A second-hand machine is hard to judge. It may not work well, and the warranty may be gone `card 5.items.7`
- You can choose your supplier. In Ontario, respiratory therapists must tell you that you can pick another provider or product, and that your care won’t suffer if you do `card 5.items.8`
- Whoever you buy from, online or in person: if you’re new to PAP, look for a provider who shows you how to use your equipment, teaches you about PAP therapy and checks in with you regularly. If you buy online, choose a company that asks for your prescription first. Some local providers won’t help with equipment bought online. `card 5.note`

## Card 6

*Group `withoutMachine` · ask `dentist` · figures: `columns`*

- Oral appliances `card 6.title`
- An oral appliance fits over your teeth. It holds your lower jaw and tongue forward so they don’t block your airway `card 6.items.1`
- It may suit mild to moderate sleep apnea, or people who can’t use or don’t want CPAP `card 6.items.2`
- Get a medical assessment first, to confirm sleep apnea and how severe it is. Your dentist should work with your doctor `card 6.items.3`
- See a dentist with experience fitting these devices. For people it works for, a custom device can work as well as CPAP and is often easier to put up with `card 6.items.4`
- Adjustable devices are better than fixed ones. Devices with rigid bars or screws likely work better than ones with elastic straps `card 6.items.5`
- Boil-and-bite devices are not recommended for treating sleep apnea. They are much less effective and more likely to cause problems `card 6.items.6`
- Jaw or tooth pain, chewing trouble and some tooth movement can happen. Tell your dentist if it isn’t comfortable `card 6.items.7`
- After you start, your doctor may order a follow-up sleep test to check it’s working `card 6.items.8`
- An oral appliance is a medical treatment, not a store-bought snoring guard. Your sleep team and your dentist decide together if it’s right for you. `card 6.note`

## Card 7

*Group `withoutMachine` · ask `team` · figures: `columns`*

- Weight and everyday habits `card 7.title`
- Mild sleep apnea with overweight or obesity: lifestyle changes are the recommended treatment `card 7.items.1`
- Mild sleep apnea without overweight: active treatment isn’t routinely recommended. But treatment should be considered if you’re tired or sleepy, even when it’s mild `card 7.items.2`
- Healthy habits help, but on their own they aren’t enough if you’re sleepy or have moderate or severe sleep apnea `card 7.items.3`
- Losing weight can shrink the tissue around your airway. People who lose 10% of their weight can have a 30% decrease in their symptoms `card 7.items.4`
- Weight loss works better if you keep using your treatment while you lose weight, then get retested once you reach your goal `card 7.items.5`
- Alcohol and sedatives (medicines that make you sleepy) can relax your throat muscles too much and let your airway close. Avoiding them may help keep it open `card 7.items.6`
- Smoking can make your nose stuffy and irritated, which can get in the way of CPAP `card 7.items.7`
- Lifestyle changes don’t work for everyone, and may not work forever. If your symptoms come back, see your doctor `card 7.items.8`
- If you gain or lose 30 pounds (about 14 kg) or more, go back to your sleep specialist. Your pressure may need to be checked `card 7.items.9`
- Habits work alongside your treatment, not instead of it, unless your sleep team says so. Don’t stop a treatment or a prescribed medicine without talking to your doctor. `card 7.note`
- Weight `card 7.figure.columns.1.heading`
- Alcohol, sedatives, smoking `card 7.figure.columns.2.heading`

## Card 8

*Group `withoutMachine` · ask `team` · figures: `columns`*

- Comparing your options `card 8.title`
- Moderate or severe sleep apnea: CPAP is the recommended treatment. If CPAP isn’t acceptable to you, an oral appliance is recommended `card 8.items.1`
- Mild sleep apnea: lifestyle changes if you’re overweight. CPAP or an oral appliance are also options `card 8.items.2`
- CPAP has the largest effect on how severe sleep apnea is `card 8.items.3`
- A jaw-advancing oral appliance has roughly a 70% chance of working in mild sleep apnea. In moderate sleep apnea it has about a 50% chance of fully working, and in severe about 40%. In moderate and severe, the chances are lower with a BMI over 35 `card 8.items.4`
- Every treatment takes getting used to. Using it every night, all night, is what makes it work `card 8.items.5`
- Whatever you choose, follow-up continues until your breathing pauses are back to normal: fewer than 5 an hour `card 8.items.6`
- Tongue-retaining devices are only for snoring or very mild sleep apnea `card 8.items.7`
- Your sleep team decides with you, based on your sleep test, your health and what you can use every night. `card 8.note`

## Card 9

*Group `withoutMachine` · ask `team`*

- Shop fixes and new treatments: check first `card 9.title`
- Nasal strips and nasal dilators may help air flow through your nose. They don’t treat the blockage in your throat that causes sleep apnea `card 9.items.1`
- A chin strap on its own doesn’t fix the blockage either. A badly fitted strap can push the jaw back and make the blockage worse `card 9.items.2`
- Boil-and-bite mouthguards are not recommended for treating sleep apnea `card 9.items.3`
- There are many experimental treatments. Before you try one, ask your sleep specialist about the risks and benefits for you `card 9.items.4`
- Snoring can be a sign of sleep apnea. If you’re treating snoring on your own, get checked first. `card 9.note`
