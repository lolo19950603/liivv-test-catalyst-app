## Card 2

*Group `yourSetup` · ask `prescriber`*

- Your prescription and settings `card 2.title`
- You need a prescription for CPAP. It comes after a medical assessment and a sleep test `card 2.items.1`
- Your prescription says what pressure your machine is set to. For insurance, it should also name your mask and humidifier `card 2.items.2`
- Auto-adjusting machines (APAP) and bilevel machines are more complex. A sleep specialist should prescribe them `card 2.items.3`
- Only trained professionals should change your machine’s settings. Don’t change them yourself `card 2.items.4`
- A machine is set for one person. Using a machine set for someone else could harm your health `card 2.items.5`
- Ask your prescriber about your pressure if your weight goes up or down by 30 pounds or more, or if you move to, or often travel to, a much higher or lower place. If you’re told you have central sleep apnea, talk to your doctor about whether to keep using CPAP `card 2.items.6`
- Every CPAP machine sold in Canada must have a medical device licence. You can look a machine up in Health Canada’s online licence list `card 2.items.7`
- In Ontario, respiratory therapists must tell you that you can choose a different provider or product `card 2.items.8`
- Liivv never advises on pressure or settings. Questions about them go to your prescriber or your respiratory therapist. `card 2.note`

## Card 4

*Group `yourSetup` · ask `rt`*

- Getting the fit right `card 4.title`
- The top of the mask sits at the bridge of your nose. The bottom sits about halfway between the bottom of your nose and your upper lip `card 4.items.1`
- The smallest mask that fits is usually the best choice `card 4.items.2`
- A little leak is fine, as long as it isn’t blowing into your eyes. You shouldn’t need to pull the straps very tight `card 4.items.3`
- When you try masks, lie on your back and on each side, with the machine attached. Make sure the headgear is easy for you to use `card 4.items.4`
- Ask about a mask trial or an exchange before you buy. Some providers don’t take returns once a mask has been used `card 4.items.5`
- Marks should fade soon after you take the mask off. If they don’t, or your skin is sore, the straps may be too tight or the mask may not fit `card 4.items.6`
- For a child, don’t fasten the mask and straps too tightly. You should be able to slip two fingers under the strap. If the mask doesn’t seem the right size, ask your child’s home care company or sleep team about other masks. `card 4.note`
- Sources: Sleep apnea (Canadian Lung Association); Positive Airway Pressure (PAP) Buyer's Guide (BC Ministry of Health); Buyer's Information for CPAP Therapy Equipment (MyHealth Alberta); Positive airway pressure (PAP): CPAP and BPAP (AboutKidsHealth, SickKids)

## Card 5

*Group `yourSetup` · ask `rt`*

- Humidifier and distilled water `card 5.title`
- Most CPAP machines have a humidifier: a small water tank that adds moisture to the air `card 5.items.1`
- Moist air can help with a dry mouth or throat, a stuffy nose and nosebleeds `card 5.items.2`
- Use only distilled water. You can buy it at most grocery stores and pharmacies `card 5.items.3`
- Put in fresh water each night. Empty the tank before you move the machine, so no water spills into it `card 5.items.4`
- Don’t put a regular room humidifier in your bedroom. It could damage the CPAP machine’s motor `card 5.items.5`
- Water drops or popping sounds in the tube? A heated tube helps stop water collecting. Ask your RT `card 5.items.6`
- You may need more moisture in winter than in summer. Ask your RT, rather than changing the settings yourself `card 5.items.7`
- Your provider will show you how to fill the water tank. Cleaning is in Everyday Liivving. `card 5.note`
- Sources: Positive Airway Pressure (PAP) Buyer's Guide (BC Ministry of Health); Sleep apnea (Canadian Lung Association); Your journey with obstructive sleep apnea: patient pathway (Provincial Pathways Unit, Primary Care Alberta, and Alberta Health Services)

## Card 7

*Group `firstWeeks` · ask `sleepTeam`*

- Your first nights `card 7.title`
- Some people sleep well with CPAP from the first night. Others need time to get used to the mask and the pressure. It can take up to 6 weeks `card 7.items.1`
- Practise first. Wear the mask for short periods during the day, sitting up and awake, before you sleep in it `card 7.items.2`
- Wear it the whole time you sleep, every night. You can wear it for naps too `card 7.items.3`
- Knocking the mask off now and then is normal. Put it back on and keep your treatment going all night `card 7.items.4`
- Many people feel less sleepy within about 2 weeks. The full benefit can take about 6 weeks `card 7.items.5`
- A bed partner may sleep better too. They can help by learning about your treatment `card 7.items.6`
- For a child, practise wearing the mask while they’re awake and doing a quiet activity they enjoy. Making it part of the bedtime routine, or a sticker chart, can help. More in This Might Be You. `card 7.note`

- What your usage numbers mean `card 8.title`
- Your machine records how many hours you use it, and on how many days `card 8.items.1`
- It may also show ‘events per hour’. That’s a useful daily sign of how therapy is going, but it isn’t the same as the AHI from a sleep test `card 8.items.2`
- Your prescriber, your insurer or a licensing body may ask for this data. When you choose a machine, ask whether it stores at least 200 days of it `card 8.items.3`
- Many machines store data on servers outside Canada, where privacy laws may be different. Ask your provider how your data is handled `card 8.items.4`

## Card 9

*Group `firstWeeks` · ask `rt` · figures: `troubleshootFinder`*

- Fix a common problem `card 9.title`
- Air leaking: a small leak that doesn’t blow into your eyes is fine. For more, check the fit and the size. Don’t over-tighten the straps `card 9.items.1`
- Dry mouth: your mouth may be falling open. A chin strap can help, and your doctor can tell you whether a full-face mask suits you. A pharmacist can suggest products for a dry mouth `card 9.items.2`
- Dry or stuffy nose: a humidifier can help. Still stuffy? See your doctor. It could be sinusitis, allergies or swelling in your nose `card 9.items.3`
- Bloating: tell your healthcare team. They may have ideas to help `card 9.items.4`
- Feeling closed in: practise wearing the mask while you’re awake. Nasal pillows or a nasal mask may feel less closed in than a full-face mask. Talking with a counsellor can help too `card 9.items.5`
- Marks or sore skin: the straps may be too tight, or the mask may not fit. Wash the mask every day, and wash and dry your face before you put it on. A rash? Call your doctor `card 9.items.6`
- Water in the tube, or popping sounds: ask your RT whether a heated tube would help. Empty the water tank before you move the machine `card 9.items.7`
- Mask coming off at night: now and then is normal. A chin strap may help keep it on `card 9.items.8`
- The air feels too strong, or it’s hard to breathe out: call your RT or prescriber. Don’t change the pressure or settings yourself `card 9.items.9`
- Still sleepy, even though you use it every night: have your supplier check that your machine is working, and talk to your sleep doctor. Sleepy while driving? Go to Driving & Staying Safe `card 9.items.10`
